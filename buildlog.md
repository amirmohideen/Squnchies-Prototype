# Build Log: Squnchies
Genre: Tower Defense & Strategy

## What to play
**The submission is `index.html` — "Squnchies: Hush Isles".** Serve the folder and open it;
it needs a local web server, because browsers block ES modules over `file://`.

Two prototypes were built during this project and both are kept in the repository. The one
that ships is the isle-defense game: three snowbound clearings, lighthouses to keep lit,
corrupted Squnchies walking out of the wood, and a purify-don't-kill loop where every boss
you win over fights beside you afterwards. A voyage runs about five minutes.

`lane.html` is the earlier lane-defense prototype. It is complete and playable, and the first
half of this log documents building it — it is kept because the decisions in it (the squeeze
mechanic, the economy, the difficulty philosophy) carried directly into the isle game. It is
not part of the submission and is excluded from the packaged zip.

The log below runs in order: Sessions 1–7 build the lane prototype, and from Session 7 onward
the isle game is designed, built and tuned into the entry.

## Decisions locked so far (lane prototype — carried into the isle game)
- Core loop: read the incoming wave → spend Squish to place/upgrade squishy toys → the board auto-squishes on its own gentle rhythm → press-and-hold a toy to charge a big squish, release on the wobble peak for a PERFECT → wave resolves, breathe, repeat. Ten waves, then the Tangle boss.
- Genre floor: placeable defenses with distinct roles, escalating waves, a spend-or-upgrade economy that stays scarce. All three present.
- Signature mechanic: towers are fidget toys you physically squeeze. Charged squishes always land in full; Knots and the boss shrug off 50% of automatic damage ("tapArmor"), so squeezing is the strategy, not decoration.
- Controls: everything is tap or press-and-hold, one-handed, portrait. Quick tap = select/upgrade; hold = squeeze.
- Scope: one Room (Bedroom Shelf), 3 toys (Squisher / Splatter / Boinger, 3 tiers each), 3 snarls (Jitter / Knot / Spark) + Tangle boss, 10 authored waves, Calm meter → Hush Pulse ultimate, win/lose/replay. Phase-2 content (more Rooms, Stretcher/Glommer, Prickler, Shelf collection, Fidget Mode) deliberately cut.
- Tech: all game code in one readable, unminified index.html, developed directly in that file (no build step, so no stale-build risk). Three.js r0.180.0 vendored locally in vendor/ (two ES-module files) via an import map. One bundled 30 KB Fredoka variable font (SIL OFL). All sound synthesized at runtime with WebAudio — zero audio files, zero network requests.
- Portrait handling: the game renders in a portrait column that pillarboxes on wide screens; the camera keeps a constant horizontal field of view so the board width fits every phone aspect.
- Difficulty philosophy (from the original GDD): gentle stakes. A tutorial-following newcomer should win while feeling competent; ignoring the squeeze should lose late; full AFK should lose early.
- Art: warm-light-in-a-dark-room palette from the pre-production Visual Concept Package; glossy gel heroes with calm faces vs spiky, jittery, coral snarls. Clear first, pretty second.

## Session 3 (2026-09-04, sitting 1): automated playtests and the balance pivot
Tool(s): Claude Code.
What I built: a headless test harness — refactored the main loop to expose a pure tick(dt) step, then scripted full-session player profiles through the real input paths: an engaged squeezer, a shopper who never squeezes, and an AFK player.
Biggest problem (and the pivot that mattered): the never-squeeze player won flawlessly — the signature mechanic was strategically irrelevant, exactly the failure the original production plan warned about. Pivoted the combat model using the GDD's own language ("a Knot soaks up taps, so it wants a charged squish"): cut auto damage ~35%, added tapArmor (Knots and the boss shrug off half of auto damage, charged always lands full, with a bright blink when a tap is shrugged), meaner waves 7–10, +9%/wave HP scaling.
What changed after playtesting: post-pivot results — AFK loses at wave 3, never-squeeze bleeds 10→2 light by wave 9 and loses to the boss, engaged squeezer wins. A full night runs about six minutes.
What I learned: automated playtests through the real game code find balance truths a designer's eyeball never will; "it feels fine" hid a dead mechanic.
Where things stand / next: the loop is fun-shaped and the squeeze matters. Next: verify every interaction end-to-end and package.

## Session 6 (2026-09-05): a second prototype direction — "Hush Isles" island defense
Tool(s): Claude Code.
What I built: a complete second playable prototype (island.html, alongside the existing lane game) translating small-island tactical defense ideas into the Squnchies universe, on the same visual system: one rotatable floating island at night, a lighthouse to protect, three one-Squnchie squads (Luma Block / Halo Puff / Prism Drop), corrupted visitors (Murks and a big Gloomp) arriving by boat from three coves across three tides. Nothing dies: light attacks drain the visible ink inside enemies, and at zero corruption they are PURIFIED — their colours return, their face wakes up, and they waddle into the lighthouse as rescued friends. Full gesture set for one thumb: tap-select + tap-move on tactical zones, hold-to-charge specials (burst / healing cloud / piercing beam), swipe-across-a-character to squish it like a stress toy (never moves it), swipe-the-water to spin the island with momentum, small vertical camera adjust. Victory/defeat/replay, boat-direction edge hints, procedural audio (sea hush, foghorn, purify chimes), all in one readable file reusing the established palette, font and HUD kit.
Key decisions: tactical zones are a hand-authored node graph blended into the terrain (not a visible grid), with pocket chokepoints on the mound; enemies never share a tile with a defender — they stop one zone short and spit ink; a swipe that starts on a character is always a squish, so toy-poking can never misfire the camera or a move order; the lighthouse slowly re-warms between tides (pressure with recovery beats, cozy not punishing).
Pivots: the first island build buried the landing coves under the grass tier — enemies would have spawned inside the terrain. Rebuilt the shoreline as a walkable ring below the grass cliff with visible sand coves. Also softened the lighthouse beam and brightened the sea after the first screenshot review.
What changed after playtesting: headless full-battle sims through the real game systems — passive play loses in tide 3 (rotating and repositioning genuinely matter), an attentive player who repositions toward incoming coves and uses specials wins with all 10 visitors rescued. A mid-battle screenshot caught the Gloomp overlapping a defender on one tile (engage range was shorter than the longest zone link) — fixed with longer spit ranges plus a hold-the-tile engagement rule; also fixed shadow z-fighting and a hit-knockback that slowly drifted defenders out of formation.
Biggest problem: gesture disambiguation on one thumb — tap, hold, squish-swipe and camera-swipe all begin with the same touch. Solved with a small state machine (movement promotes a unit-press to squish; stillness promotes it to charging; empty-space presses own the camera) and verified every path with dispatched pointer events.
What I learned: "rotate to see the other side" only creates strategy if threats actually exploit it — the west-cove wave that lands behind the lighthouse is what makes the spin matter.
Where things stand / next: one polished battle is fully playable. Next: real-phone feel pass (spin inertia, squish sensitivity), then decide which prototype leads the submission — and if it's this one, it becomes index.html and the docs update to match.

## Session 7 (2026-09-05, second sitting): feedback round against the reference
Tool(s): Claude Code.
What changed after playtesting: played the build against reference screenshots of the genre's benchmark island-defense game and gave four notes — the camera was too close to ever see the whole island; squads should be BOUGHT bigger (not just moved); the random water ripples looked wrong next to the reference's shoreline foam; and the island itself should be an irregular terraced headland rather than a round cake, keeping the cozy night palette but brighter.
What I built in response: (1) the Glow economy — purifying a visitor now yields Glow ✨, and the three bottom portraits became a squad shop: tap to add an identical member (up to four; costs 20/35/50), the whole squad shares one zone, moves as one, shrinks slightly per member so everyone stays pokeable, and every member attacks — this is also the genre's required "spend decision that matters", which the island prototype had been missing. (2) Camera pulled back and steepened so the entire island sits in frame with open sea around it (then nudged back in after it overshot to "too far"). (3) The island rebuilt as two irregular catmull-rom blob terraces extruded with pale chalk-lavender cliff walls and dusky-moss tops, sand coves tucked against the cliff, steps carved to each tier — the reference's silhouette in the Squnchies night palette, with lights and terrain brightened. (4) Shore waves: two foam outlines that hug the island's actual blob shape and breathe outward on a loop, replacing the random ripple spawner (boat-wake ripples kept).
Key decisions: selection now happens only by tapping the creature itself (portraits are purely the shop); member counts show as pips on each portrait with the next cost.
Biggest problem: reshaping terrain around an existing node graph — two rear zones fell outside the first blob footprint, caught by checking every node's containment against the sampled outline and nudging both inward.
What I learned: framing IS game design on a phone — the same battle felt cramped and unreadable close-up, and tactical the moment the whole diorama fit on screen.

## Session 9 (2026-09-05, fourth sitting): a four-isle campaign with a chart between nights
Tool(s): Claude Code.
What I built: turned the single battle into a short voyage. Four hand-built isles, each with its own coastline, terraces, tactical zones, tides and fixed weather — Hush Isle (clear, one lamp), Rainward Spit (rain, a long thin spit exposed on both flanks), Frostpin Shoal (snow, two lobes joined by an isthmus, two lamps), and The Dreaming Rock (dream-motes, a three-tier spire, two lamps at different heights). Clearing an isle opens a sea chart — a drawn map with a dotted sailing route, lit beacons on cleared isles, the next one glowing, later ones locked — where you pick your next night. Squads and Glow travel with you between isles, so the shop is now a campaign-long investment rather than a per-battle one.
Key decisions, all three checked against the competition rules first: (1) Weather is FIXED per isle, never a cycle that shifts mid-battle — the guidance explicitly rules out dynamic weather but allows a fixed weather state. (2) The whole campaign is one sitting (~6.5 minutes) with no save system, because judges play a single session and "anything that only unlocks later will not be seen". (3) Four complete isles rather than eight sketchy ones, because Focus penalises half-built systems. Multiple lighthouses also strengthen the genre floor: you lose an isle only when every lamp goes dark, so losing one and holding the rest is a real, recoverable moment.
Pivots: my first difficulty pass was wrong in both directions. Squads carrying over meant they hit 4/4/4 by isle three and the last two isles became trivial (405 Glow left unspent, lamps never scratched). I made squad damage sublinear — a second friend is a big jump, a fourth a top-up — added a per-isle enemy scale, and grew the late tides. Then the finale overshot to unwinnable, and dialling its scale from 2.5 → 2.0 → 1.6 landed it.
Biggest problem: rebuilding four isles broke the tactical layouts in ways screenshots hid — zones buried inside raised plateaus, standing in a lighthouse's base, or overhanging a cliff. Extended the invariant checker to cover terrace containment, terrace-lip clearance, zone-to-zone spacing and zone-to-lighthouse spacing, then wrote a relaxation solver that nudges zones apart while holding them on the correct terrace and printed the settled coordinates to paste back. It solved isles 1–2 outright and proved isles 3–4 were unsolvable as drawn — their plateaus were simply too small for the zones I had put on them — which told me to redesign those two with roomier terraces and one guard zone per lamp rather than keep nudging numbers.
What I learned: when a solver cannot satisfy your constraints, that is design feedback, not a bug. It said the plateaus were too small before I could see it.

## Session 10 (2026-09-06): cutting the menu — the ferry between isles
Tool(s): Claude Code.
What changed after playtesting: the chart screen I had added between isles was a menu, and it broke the spell — you finished a night and then had to read a list and tap a button. Replaced it with a sailing sequence, so the campaign is now one unbroken loop with no menu anywhere between the first tap and the last isle.
What I built: when a night is won a ferry noses up to the shore, your Squnchies file aboard one at a time (staggered arcs, each one shrinking as it drops onto the deck), the ferry pulls away, the screen dips to dark, and it sails back in toward the next isle where they hop off onto their starting posts and the night begins. Under the darkness the whole isle is swapped and the squads rebuilt aboard, so the seam is invisible. During the crossing a card rides along showing where you're sailing to, the isle's weather, your roster and Glow, and the sea chart with a little ship moving along the dotted route — a picture, not a menu; it never waits for input. The chart drawing was kept and repurposed, so nothing was thrown away: it now serves both the crossing card and the final screen.
Key decisions: the only two screens left are the two real endings — the whole voyage won, or a night lost (which offers a retry of that isle with exactly what you arrived carrying). The ferry itself is drawn as an extruded boat outline with a pointed bow, an open deck, a cream sail and a honey pennant laid almost flat, because from the game's high camera a normal sail is edge-on and reads as nothing — the first version looked like a grey oval.
Biggest problem: a stale win-timer. Each win schedules the ferry 1.9 seconds later; if the campaign ended or the player restarted inside that window, the timer could still fire and ask to sail past the last isle, which crashed the chart drawing every frame. Fixed in two places at once — the timer now checks it is still standing on the isle whose win it belongs to, and both `Voyage.begin` and the chart refuse out-of-range isles. Worth noting a debugging trap: the browser console kept replaying that crash from its buffer long after the fix, with line numbers that no longer matched the file. An in-page error listener during a live run reported zero, and the stale line number gave it away.
What I learned: verify a fix against the running page, not the console history — a buffered stack trace from an old build looks exactly like a live one.

## Session 8 (2026-09-05, third sitting): camera, landings, island shape, reflections
Tool(s): Claude Code (switched models mid-session; the build log carried the context across, which is exactly what it is for).
What changed after playtesting: a second pass against the reference screenshots produced seven notes — hint popups sat in the middle of the play area; vertical swipe didn't zoom out far enough; no pinch zoom; no pause; the three fixed sand ports felt artificial (enemies should come ashore anywhere); the island was too small and too circular; and the zone markers were single-character circles rather than squad-sized patches. Plus a request for water reflections.
What I built: (1) Toasts moved to a top strip under the HUD so nothing covers the diorama. (2) Replaced the camera's height-offset with a proper zoom factor — vertical swipe and two-finger pinch both drive it, over a 0.78×–2.0× range, so the isle can be pushed right out to see every approaching boat. (3) A pause/resume button with a dimmed "paused" overlay that also freezes input. (4) Deleted the three fixed coves entirely: each boat now picks a random heading inside its wave's arc, samples the actual coastline radius in that direction, beaches there, and its passengers hop straight onto the nearest low zone — so no two nights land the same way and spinning the island is genuinely necessary. (5) The island rebuilt again: straight-edged angular polygons instead of smoothed blobs, a much larger irregular footprint with headlands and a shallow bay, a chunky raised back plateau with taller cliffs, a cut stair, and three small tree-covered scenery islets offshore. (6) Zone markers became large nine-sided patches sized to hold a full four-Squnchie squad. (7) Reflections: procedurally drawn canvas gradient textures (no image files) give the lighthouse a soft shimmering streak on the water that slides as the isle turns and flickers when the light is failing, plus a purple lantern glow riding the water under every enemy boat.

Three rounds of that found and fixed five bad placements.
What I learned: when terrain and layout are edited separately, invariants beat eyeballing — a fifteen-line checker caught things screenshots did not.
Where things stand / next: all gestures verified through dispatched pointer events (swipe-up zoom 1.0→1.74, pinch 1.0→2.0, pause freezes the clock, replay resets framing). ## Session 12 (2026-09-07): grades not headcounts, squishing as the attack, and two bosses who change sides
Tool(s): Claude Code.
What changed after playtesting: four notes, and the first two are the same note. Buying extra squad members made squads *smaller* (they shrank to share a zone), so spending Glow made the toys less pokeable — backwards for a game about squeezing them. And the signature squish was still pure decoration: it wobbled and did nothing, so there was no reason to keep doing it once the novelty wore off. Also: the Luma Block was a sphere despite being called a block, and the enemies were anonymous blobs with no health readout.
What I built: (1) **Grades replace headcount.** Every squad is now one Squnchie that grows Base → Pro → Max. A grade buys four things at once — size, health, reach, and the number of bolts thrown per volley (1/2/3), so an upgrade is visible in the silhouette, in the range ring and in the air. (2) **Squishing is the attack.** Wringing a Squnchie makes it throw its light immediately, scaled by how far you actually stretched it (0.85× to 2.0×) on a half-second cooldown. The fidget is now the strategy. (3) **New silhouettes**, all from one shared builder so a Murk is literally a small boss: the Luma Block is at last a rounded cube; the Halo Puff took over the old plump sphere and kept its ring; the corrupted wear a cat's head, and after the first boss falls, a soft-serve cone. (4) **Two bosses that join you.** The Mewl (cat) arrives on isle 2, the Swirl (cone) closes the voyage. Purify one and it does not walk into the lighthouse — it takes a free zone, keeps its exact silhouette lit from within, joins the shop row, and fights the next tide beside you. Its type is inferred from the shape of its `special` block, so a converted boss slots into the existing special system without a single special case. (5) Ink meters over every visitor, camera-aligned each frame.
Key decisions: the ordinary tide always wears the silhouette of whichever boss is still at large, so a wave is a warning about what is coming — and the moment the Mewl changes sides, the sea starts sending cones. Extra beams in a prism fan buy coverage, never stacked damage: near the caster the beams overlap, so each visitor is counted once however many cross it. Boss ink is the authored number and is never multiplied by the isle's enemy scale, because a boss *is* the fight and should be tuned by hand.
Biggest problem: the first balance pass had bosses melting in two seconds — 430 ink gone before the banner cleared. Two causes. The beam fan was multi-hitting a single target three times at close range, and the boss numbers were simply too low. Fixed the fan, raised the bosses to 900 and 2200 ink, and re-measured: 11 seconds at the bot's aggressive ceiling. The economy was also inflated (645 Glow unspent, everything Max by the finale), so grades went to 70/150 and per-visitor Glow came down ~20%; the voyage now ends with the last squad still one grade short.
Verification: a scripted run that never squishes **loses on isle 3** with lamps at 2 HP, while the same run squishing wins with lamps barely scratched — the mechanic is necessary, not merely faster. The same contrast shows in the boss fight itself: 33 seconds to purify the Mewl without squishing, 11 with. Full campaign 429s, zero live errors, all four isles pass every geometry invariant, converted bosses always land on an unclaimed zone, and the build still makes exactly four local network requests.
What I learned: an upgrade should be visible in the silhouette. Making squads shrink as they grew was a small, reasonable-looking decision that quietly worked against the entire premise of the game, and no amount of number-tuning would have fixed it.
Post-switch addition: a resting Squnchie now carries a recovery meter above its head, so you can see how long until it is back rather than guessing. It fills rather than drains — glancing at the board should read "nearly back", not "nearly gone" — and it warms from mint to honey as it approaches waking. It shares one factory with the visitors' ink meters and the same camera-alignment, and its scale is divided by the squad's grade so a Max Squnchie's meter is the same size on screen as a Base one; only its height off the ground follows the body. It exists only while the toy is soaked, so a heal that wakes one early simply removes it.
Gave the ending screens room to breathe: the gap under the message went 18px → 38px and the gap under the stats 28px → 42px, so the screen reads as three blocks — what happened, how it went, what to do next — rather than one stack of text running into the button. Checked at 375×667, the smallest screen worth worrying about, with the won screen's trail map in place: everything fits with over a hundred pixels clear top and bottom, and neither ending scrolls.

The shop row now carries ONE dimmed, dashed slot for the ally you can win tonight — four cards on the first isle, five on the second, and five on the third with no slot at all, because that boss is won as the voyage ends and a promise with nowhere left to spend it is worse than no promise. Dropping the extra slots also gave the cards real room back: 88px each on isle 1 against the 57px they had when the whole campaign was laid out at once.
This is the design guidance's "progression within a single session" made legible in the first ten seconds rather than something a judge has to infer. It is worth being precise about why it does not fall foul of the neighbouring warning — "anything that only unlocks later will not be seen" — which is about content gated behind SESSIONS. Measured across two runs, the first ally is won at about 70 seconds and the second at about 171, inside a voyage of roughly four and a half minutes, so every slot on the row is filled during a single sitting.
Key decision: the slot says `?` and "tonight", not the character's name. That there is somebody to win this isle is a promise the player can act on; who it is stays the surprise. Because exactly one boss falls per isle, the next unwon ally is always the one on the current isle, so "tonight" is always literally true and reads better than repeating the isle number already in the header. What changed after playtesting: the strongest remaining resemblance to the genre's best-known example was the *setting* — an island in an ocean with enemies arriving by boat. That is the silhouette people recognise, and no amount of mechanical divergence underneath it changes the first impression. So the water went. Also: the tide counter was a line of text where a bar would read better.
What I built: (1) **The sea is now a snowfield.** The same terrain, the same isles, but the surface around them is pale moonlit snow rather than dark water — matte, no metalness, because snow scatters light instead of reflecting it. The lighthouse no longer throws a mirror-streak back at the camera; it lays a soft wedge of lit ground that sweeps as the lamp turns. (2) **A wood that runs to the horizon.** 780 trees in two InstancedMeshes — one for every trunk, one for every crown, so the whole forest is two draw calls. They are spread by the square root of a uniform sample so they cover ground evenly instead of crowding the treeline, and they thicken with distance, so zooming out finds more forest rather than an edge. (3) **The corrupted walk in.** Boats are gone entirely. A `Band` is now an invisible marker that walks in from the treeline with its members trudging behind it in a staggered file; at the isle's rim they peel off one at a time and start climbing. (4) **Tracks in the snow**, pressed at every landing — on the field and on the isle alike — holding for three seconds and then fading as fresh snow fills them in, so you can read where a tide came from. (5) The ferry became a **sled**: a flat bed on curled runners, the same honey pennant, spray behind it instead of a wake. (6) The tide counter became a **progress bar** with one tick per tide.
